Barkley vs Brown

Two of the best-known explanations of executive function. They are not rivals — they answer different questions, and most people need both.

If you have read about executive function for any length of time you have met both names, usually without being told how they fit together. They are not competing theories to pick between. They answer two different questions, and the questions are ones people actually ask out loud:

The question Barkley answers

“Why can’t I make myself start, when I know exactly what to do?”

Barkley’s answer is that executive function is one system for managing yourself over time, and it has a gate: the ability to pause. Knowing the right thing and being able to act on it are different jobs, handled differently in the brain. That is why knowledge alone never fixes it — and why building supports into your environment is not cheating.

Read the full Barkley guide →

The question Brown answers

“If I can do it sometimes, why can’t I do it all the time?”

Brown’s answer is that executive function is not one dial but six clusters, and they vary sharply by context, interest, and reward. The same person can lose four hours to something absorbing and be unable to start a ten-minute task. That pattern is a description of how the system works, not a verdict on your character.

The short version

Barkley explains why it breaks down. Brown describes what it looks like when it does. Useful work generally needs both: a reason it is happening that does not reduce to laziness, and a specific enough picture of your own pattern to know where to start.
